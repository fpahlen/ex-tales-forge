# The Shadowed Linage

### Chapter 1: The Light-Fingered Fool

The market square of Harveston stank of wet straw and hot bread and the sour promise of rain that never quite fell. Gav had known that smell since the day he could crawl—knew it better than his own name, which was all anyone had ever given him. Gavin, they called him once, some priest with a pinched mouth and colder hands, but the streets had shortened it to Gav and the name had stuck like mud on boots. He was fifteen, thin as a ferret, with hair the color of old straw and eyes the color of nothing much at all. They watched everything and gave away nothing.

He perched on the edge of a fishmonger’s cart, legs dangling, one hand absently scratching at the flea bites beneath his ragged tunic. The square was thick with bodies: farmers hauling sacks of grain, halfling peddlers hawking trinkets from burrow-carts, a pair of Montfort guards in green-and-gold livery leaning against a tavern wall and pretending to watch for trouble. Gav knew their faces. They knew his. They had an understanding: he didn’t cut purses too close to the guardhouse, and they didn’t chase him too hard when he did.

Today the crowd was heavier than usual. Word had come down from the royal palace that King Clovis IV would be riding out to bless the new harvest festival. The boy-king had been crowned five years ago and still wore the Sacred Crown like a child playing at being a man. Gav had seen him once, from a rooftop—golden hair, pale cheeks, eyes that darted like a hare’s. The sight had left him unimpressed. Kings were just men with more knives pointed at their backs.

Gav’s stomach growled. He had eaten nothing since yesterday’s half-loaf and the crust had been hard enough to break teeth. Time to work.

He slid off the cart, slipped between two matrons arguing over the price of apples, and scanned the marks. The usual: fat merchants with bulging purses, farmers with leather bags tied clumsily at their belts, the occasional fool in silk who thought the city belonged to him.

Then he saw her.

She stood near the fountain, half-hidden by the spray. Dark hair pinned beneath a hooded cloak the color of wet slate. No jewelry, no flash—nothing to draw the eye except the way she moved, deliberate and quiet, like a cat in a room full of dogs. A broker, Gav thought. One of those who traded in whispers and sealed letters instead of coin. She had the look of someone who knew too much and carried too little.

Gav’s fingers itched.

He drifted closer, letting the crowd carry him. A baker’s boy shoved past with a tray of rolls; Gav used the moment to brush against the woman’s side, light as a sigh. The purse was small, leather, tied with a thin cord. He cut it with the edge of a broken thumbnail, caught the weight in his palm, and melted back into the press of bodies before she could turn.

He didn’t run. Running was for amateurs. He walked, head down, heart drumming against his ribs, until he reached the narrow alley behind the tanner’s shop. There he stopped, pressed his back to the wall, and opened the purse.

Coins first—three silver stags and a handful of coppers. Enough for bread and a bed for a week. Then paper. A folded sheet of parchment, sealed with black wax and the sigil of a grey crescent moon.

Gav’s mouth went dry.

The Grey Consortium. Everyone knew the name, even if no one spoke it aloud. Merchants, they called themselves. Logistics specialists. The kind of people who could make a man disappear between one breath and the next. Gav had seen their shades before—quiet men in plain cloaks who moved through the market like smoke and left no tracks. He had never taken one before. That was suicide.

He should have dropped the purse and walked away.

Instead he broke the seal.

The letter inside was written in a careful hand, the ink still faintly warm.

*To the Factor in Whisperdale:*

*The Marguerite File has been authenticated. The portrait is genuine. Pointed ears, unmistakable. The old woman is half-elf, the cousins quarter-blood. If the Beaumonts fall, Vallombre and Montfort fall with them. The documents must reach Lady Serina before the Winter Ball. Payment upon delivery. No mistakes.*

*Shade Seven*

Gav stared at the words until they blurred. Marguerite. The grandmother who lived in Thornhaven Manor like a ghost. The Beaumonts—Garrick and Nyra—were the only two barons who didn’t spend their time trying to eat each other alive. If this was true, if they had elven blood…

He folded the letter with shaking hands. This wasn’t a purse full of coin. This was a knife in the dark, aimed at two of the most powerful people in the kingdom.

Footsteps.

Gav shoved the letter into his tunic and pressed deeper into the alley. The fishmonger’s cart rattled past, then stopped. A shadow fell across the mouth of the alley—tall, cloaked, the same slate-grey as the woman’s.

She had noticed.

Gav bolted.

He didn’t think. He ran.

The alley twisted, narrow and slick with runoff. He took the first corner at full speed, boots sliding on wet cobbles, then launched himself at the wall. His fingers found purchase on a broken shutter; he swung up, scrambled onto the low roof of the tanner’s shop, and kept moving. Below, the woman shouted—once, sharp and cold. Then silence.

Gav ran the rooftops the way other boys ran the streets. He had learned the paths years ago: the gap over the butcher’s yard, the slant of the cooper’s shed, the chimney stack that leaned just far enough to jump to the next building. His lungs burned. His legs burned. But he was fast—faster than anyone who chased him—and he knew the city like his own skin.

He dropped into a courtyard behind a tavern, rolled under a cart, and lay still. The woman’s footsteps passed overhead, paused, then faded.

Gav waited until the bells of the harvest festival began to ring, then crawled out. His heart still hammered. The letter burned against his chest like a brand.

He needed to think.

The obvious thing was to sell it. There were always buyers for secrets in Harveston. A rival baron. A merchant with a grudge. The Grey Consortium itself, if he could find a way to reach them without losing his head.

But selling it meant choosing a side. And Gav had never chosen sides before. Sides got you killed.

He walked to the river, to the place beneath the old Aurelian bridge where the water ran black and slow. There he sat on the mossy stone and took out the letter again. The words stared back at him.

*Pointed ears. Unmistakable.*

He had heard the rumors—everyone had. The Beaumonts were too clean, too clever, too untouchable. People said they had friends in places no human should have friends. Now it seemed the rumors had teeth.

Gav folded the parchment and tucked it away. He would keep it for now. Knowledge was currency. He could decide later what to do with it.

He stood.

A man waited at the end of the bridge.

Plain cloak. Plain face. The kind of face you forgot the moment you looked away. He held no weapon, but his hands were empty in a way that made Gav’s stomach twist.

“You have something that does not belong to you,” the man said. His voice was soft, almost kind.

Gav’s mouth went dry again. “I don’t know what you’re talking about.”

The man smiled. It was a good smile—warm, understanding. The kind of smile a father might give a wayward son. “The purse. The letter. You’re quick, boy. Very quick. But quick is not the same as safe.”

Gav took a step back. The river was behind him. No rooftops here. No escape.

“I didn’t read it,” he lied.

The man tilted his head. “You did. I can see it in your eyes. You read it, and now you know something you should not. That makes you dangerous.”

Gav’s mind raced. He could jump into the river. The current was strong. He might make it to the far bank before they fished him out.

The man took a step forward. “There is no need for this to end badly. Give me the letter. Take the purse. Walk away. You’ll be richer than you were this morning, and still breathing.”

Gav’s hand went to the purse at his belt. The coins clinked softly.

“And if I don’t?”

The man’s smile never wavered. “Then you will be breathing for a much shorter time.”

Gav looked past him. Two more figures had appeared at the bridge’s far end—silent, cloaked, hands on knife hilts. Shades. Real ones.

He had seconds.

He smiled—his best smile, the one that had talked him out of beatings and into extra bread. “You know,” he said, “I always wondered what it would feel like to hold something the Consortium wanted. Turns out it feels like standing in a bear trap with your foot already caught.”

The man laughed—quiet, genuine. “You’re clever. I like that. Lady Serina likes clever boys. She might even find a place for you, if you’re wise.”

Gav’s stomach lurched. Lady Serina. The Factor herself. The name was a whisper in the dark corners of Harveston. No one saw her and lived to brag about it.

“I’m just a thief,” Gav said. “I don’t want a place. I want to eat.”

“Then give me the letter,” the man said, “and eat well tonight.”

Gav hesitated.

The man’s smile faded. “Last chance, boy.”

Gav looked down at the purse in his hand. Three silver stags. A week of bread and beds. A week of safety.

He looked up at the man’s face—still kind, still patient.

And he made his choice.

He threw the purse.

It arced high, glinting in the weak sun. The man lunged for it. Gav spun and sprinted for the riverbank.

He hit the water hard, the cold punching the breath from his lungs. The current took him immediately—fast, black, merciless. He kicked once, twice, then let it carry him downstream.

Behind him, he heard shouts. A splash. A curse.

He surfaced long enough to see the bridge receding, the three shades staring after him. One of them raised a hand—not in farewell.

Gav went under again.

When he finally clawed his way onto the muddy bank a mile downstream, he was shivering, soaked, and still clutching the letter inside his tunic.

He lay on his back, staring at the grey sky.

He had just stolen from the Grey Consortium.

He had just refused to give it back.

And now they knew his face.

Gav closed his eyes.

Somewhere in the city above, Lady Serina Vex would be hearing about this. Somewhere in the shadows, knives were already being sharpened.

And somewhere in Thornhaven Manor, an old woman with pointed ears was still alive—still dangerous.

Gav opened his eyes.

He was no longer just a thief.

He was a fool who had stepped into the dark.

And the dark had noticed.

### Chapter 2: Shadows in the Manor

The mud of the riverbank clung to Gav's boots like a beggar's grasp, sucking at each step as he trudged toward the eastern hills. Harveston lay behind him now, its bells fading into a distant clamor, but the city's eyes felt closer than ever. He had ditched the purse—tossed the coins into the black water as an offering to whatever gods watched over fools—but the letter remained, folded tight against his skin, its words burning like a fever. *Pointed ears. The old woman is half-elf.* If that was truth, then the Beaumonts were built on a lie, and lies like that could topple baronies.

Gav's breath came in ragged gasps, his thin frame shivering in the damp chill that rolled off the plains. He was no stranger to running — thief-running over rooftops, sprints through alleys thick with the stink of tanneries and butchers—but this was different. This wasn't some guard with a club and a grudge; this was the Grey Consortium, the shadows that whispered in Montfort's markets and vanished bodies into the fog. Lady Serina Vex, they said, could smell a secret from across the kingdom, and now she had his scent.

He kept to the hedgerows, skirting the main roads where caravans rumbled toward the harvest festival. Farmers in rough wool eyed him warily as he passed, their carts laden with grain sacks that would fill Baron Garrick Beaumont's granaries. Gav had always envied those carts—simple lives, bound to the earth, not the knife's edge. But the earth had its own knives; he knew that from the orphanage tales of Beaumont justice, swift and unseen. *Roots in the earth, eyes in the shadow,* their words went. Now those eyes might be on him.

The sun dipped low, painting the hills in blood and gold, when Thornhaven Manor came into view. It squatted on a rise like an old hound, grey stone half-swallowed by ivy, its watchtower peering into both Montfort and Vallombre like a suspicious neighbor. Officially abandoned, they called it—too costly to maintain, its halls echoing with the ghosts of old Beaumont lords. But Gav had heard the whispers in the taverns: it was no ruin. Servants came and went by night, and twice a year, shadows gathered there. The grandmother's lair, some said. Lady Marguerite, the spider at the web's center.

Gav crouched in the brambles, watching. No lights burned in the windows, but smoke curled thin from a hidden chimney. Guards? None visible, but that meant nothing. The Beaumonts didn't need steel at their gates; their protection was in the unseen—in deals struck in dark rooms, in poisons that tasted like wine.

His stomach twisted. Why had he come here? To warn them? To sell the letter? Gav snorted at his own foolishness. He was a pickpocket, not a player in barons' games. But the shades had his face now, and the streets of Harveston would be a deathtrap come dawn. Thornhaven was neutral ground, or so the rumors went—a place even the Consortium trod lightly. If he could slip in, hide the letter, perhaps barter it for a horse or a hood to flee east toward Rochedur's quarries...

A twig snapped behind him.

Gav whirled, but too late. A hand clamped over his mouth—rough, callused, smelling of herbs and earth. Another arm pinned his wrists. He kicked, twisted, but the grip was iron.

"Easy, lad," a voice growled, low and gravelly like stones in a mill. "Struggle and you'll wake the dead. And the living are worse."

Gav went still. The man was old, his breath wheezing, but strong as an ox. A servant? A guard? Through the fingers, Gav mumbled, "Let go. I ain't here to steal."

A chuckle, dry as autumn leaves. "Everyone steals something here. Question is, what're you after?"

The hand released his mouth, but the arm stayed firm. Gav twisted his head, catching a glimpse: weathered face, white hair cropped short, eyes like chips of flint. An old retainer, perhaps, with the green-and-gold trim of Beaumont livery peeking from beneath a cloak.

"I... I have something," Gav said, thinking fast. His charm was his best tool—better than fingers or feet. He flashed a grin, the one that had coaxed extra scraps from cooks. "Something your lady might want. A letter. Important-like."

The old man's eyes narrowed. "My lady? Which one?"

Gav blinked. *Which one?* Marguerite, of course. But then he remembered: Nyra in Vallombre, the shadow baroness. Garrick here in Montfort. Cousins, bound by blood and secrets. "The old one," he hedged. "Lady Marguerite."

The man studied him, then nodded once. "Come on, then. But try anything, and I'll gut you like a fish."

He released Gav, who rubbed his wrists and followed warily. They skirted the manor's wall, slipping through a servants' gate hidden by ivy. Inside, the grounds were overgrown—herb gardens wild with thyme and nightshade, paths cracked with weeds. But the air hummed with quiet purpose: a stable hand curried a horse in the shadows, a maid hurried with a tray of covered dishes. No one looked twice at them.

The old man led him through a side door into halls that smelled of beeswax and old stone. Torchlight flickered on tapestries faded with age: wheat sheaves crossed with daggers, the Beaumont sigil. Gav's boots left muddy prints on the flagstones, and he winced at each squelch. From mud to marble—or near enough. The orphanage floors had been dirt; this was wealth, even in decay.

They climbed a spiral stair to a solar high in the tower. The door creaked open on a room warmed by a fire in a massive hearth. Bookshelves lined the walls, laden with tomes that whispered of secrets. At a table sat a woman—old, frail as parchment, but with eyes sharp as thorns. Lady Marguerite Beaumont. Her hair was silver, pinned with combs of bone, and her gown was simple wool, but the way she sat spoke of command. Beside her, a cup of tea steamed, its scent herbal and bitter.

"Tomlin," she said, voice like rustling leaves. "What stray have you brought me?"

The old man—Tomlin—bowed. "Boy says he has a letter for you, m'lady. Claims it's important."

Marguerite's gaze fixed on Gav. He felt stripped bare, as if she could see the letter through his tunic, see the river mud on his soul. But he met her eyes, forcing a smile. "Aye, milady. Found it in the market. Thought you might... appreciate it."

Her lips twitched—amusement? Suspicion? "Found it. How quaint. Sit, boy. And speak truth, or Tomlin will show you the door—the hard way."

Gav sat, the chair hard beneath him. The fire crackled, popping like distant thunder. He pulled the letter from his tunic, its edges damp, and slid it across the table. Marguerite broke the remnants of the seal without flinching, unfolded it, and read.

Her face changed not at all. No widening eyes, no gasp. Just a slow nod, as if confirming a long-held suspicion. "The Grey Consortium," she murmured. "Persistent as rats. And you, boy—how did you come by this?"

Gav swallowed. Truth? Part of it. "Cut a purse, milady. Didn't know whose it was till I read it. Then... they came after me. Shades. I ran. Ended up here."

She set the letter down, folding her hands. Her ears—were they pointed? Gav peered, but her hair veiled them artfully. "And why bring it to me? Coin? Favor?"

He shrugged, playing the streetwise waif. "Safety, mostly. Consortium's got long arms. Figured your... roots might protect a lad like me."

A faint smile. "Roots. How poetic. Tomlin, fetch wine. The boy looks half-drowned."

Tomlin poured from a decanter—red as blood, sour on the tongue. Gav sipped, the warmth spreading through him like a lie. It tasted nothing like the watered ale of taverns; this was courtly stuff, laced with spices that hid... what? Poison? He set the cup down carefully.

Marguerite watched him. "You know what this means, then. The 'old woman' with pointed ears."

Gav nodded. "Elven blood. Means you—and the barons—ain't pure human. If it gets out..."

"It changes nothing," she said sharply. "Blood is blood. But fools with crowns and sigils care for purity like dogs for bones. The Consortium wants us weakened—Nyra's shadows compete with theirs, Garrick's grain feeds armies they can't control."

Gav leaned forward, curiosity overriding fear. "So it's true? Elves in the family tree?"

She hesitated, then lifted a strand of hair. There—subtle, but unmistakable: the tip of her ear curved to a gentle point. "My mother, Aelindra of Sylvaine's deep woods. She lives still, hidden. Love crossed borders once. Now it's a weapon."

Gav's world tilted. Elves—real ones, not tavern tales of immortal beauties in forbidden forests. And here, in a human manor, tangled in barons' games. His simple life of purses and rooftops felt distant as a dream. "Why tell me? I could sell this."

"You could," Marguerite agreed. "But you won't. Because the Consortium knows your face now, and they'll kill you for what you carry. Stay here, boy. Serve. Learn. In time, perhaps you'll be useful."

Useful. Like a tool. Gav's gut clenched. He wasn't naive enough to trust this—charismatic, yes, this old woman with her sharp eyes and hidden ears. But ruthless? The stories said yes: rivals vanished, debts called in whispers. She acted through intermediates—Tomlin, servants, shades of her own. Was he the next?

A knock at the door. Tomlin opened it, admitting a woman cloaked in shadow. Dark hair, pale face—Baroness Nyra Beaumont herself? No, too plain. A messenger, perhaps.

"Milady," the woman said, voice low. "Word from Whisperdale. The portrait—the one with the ears—it's surfaced. In Montfort. Consortium's moving to buy it."

Marguerite's face hardened. "Then we move faster. Send to Garrick. He must intercept."

The woman nodded, glanced at Gav curiously, then slipped away.

Gav's heart pounded. The portrait—from the letter. He was in it now, deep as the river's current.

"Go with Tomlin," Marguerite said. "Rest. Tomorrow, you'll earn your keep."

Tomlin led him to a servant's room—straw pallet, basin of water. Better than the streets. But as Gav lay there, the letter's words echoed. *The cousins quarter-blood.* And now he was their tool.

Sleep came fitful, dreams of shades and pointed ears. He woke to a hand over his mouth again—this time, not Tomlin's.

"Quiet," a voice hissed. Female, urgent. "I'm here to get you out."

Gav's eyes adjusted. The woman from before—the messenger? No. Hooded, but her features sharp, elven-sharp. Half-elf, perhaps.

"Who—" he started.

"No time. The old woman's using you. The letter? It's bait. Consortium planted it, knowing a thief would bite. Now you're marked for both sides."

Gav sat up, mind reeling. "Why help me?"

She smiled thinly. "Because I serve another. Baron Alain de Lumière. He wants the Beaumonts down—his knights need their lands. Come with me, or die here when the knives come."

A dilemma: stay with Marguerite, the charismatic spider, or flee with this stranger, into Lumière's radiant but ruthless grasp? Trust eroded like riverbank mud. The clash—his grounded roots screaming to run, but the intrigue's opulence whispering stay.

He grabbed his boots. "Lead on."

They slipped into the hall, shadows lengthening like lies. But at the stair, footsteps rose—Tomlin, or worse.

The woman drew a dagger. "Run or fight?"

Gav chose run—always run. He bolted for the window, leaping to the ivy below.

Behind, shouts. A curse.

The night swallowed him, but the web tightened.

### Chapter 3: Knives in the Fog

The fog of Vallombre's valleys clung to Gav like a shroud, thick and cold as the lies that had dragged him here. Three days since fleeing Thornhaven, three days of hard riding on stolen horses, dodging patrols that seemed to know his scent. The elven-featured woman—Elyse, she called herself, though Gav doubted it was true—rode ahead, her cloak blending with the mist like she was born to it. Half-elf kin, perhaps, like the rumors of Nyra's advisors. But serving Lumière? That stank of deeper games.

Gav's thighs ached from the saddle, his hands raw from reins. The clash jarred him still: from Harveston's mud and sour ale to this—poisoned wine in manors, whispers of elven blood. His practical roots rebelled; he was no courtier, no piece in barons' chess. Yet here he was, fleeing one web into another, the letter still hidden in his boot, its words a curse he couldn't shake.

They crested a ridge, and Whisperdale sprawled below: a labyrinth of alleys and illusory facades, buildings shifting in the mist like dreams. Nyra Beaumont's seat, where shadows ruled and eyes watched from every fog bank. Elyse reined in. "We skirt the town. Lumière's agents wait in the ruins beyond."

Gav eyed her. "And what does your baron want with me? I'm just a thief."

She smiled, subtext sharp as a stiletto. "Thieves have uses. You know the secret now. Proof against the Beaumonts—elven taint in their line. Alain will pay well for it."

Pay. The word tempted, but Gav's cynicism whispered caution. Lumière's chivalrous knights were charismatic masks for ruthless ambition; stories told of rivals blinded by "radiant" magic, courts undermined by golden lies. And Alain acted through intermediates—like Elyse—until the knife was needed.

They descended into the fog, the path hidden, known only to locals. Orc camps flickered in the distance, beast-tamers with their shadowed magic. Gav's heart quickened; orcs were seclusion-bound, hostile, but Nyra's allies. If they spotted intruders...

A rustle in the gorse. Elyse froze. Then, from the mist, figures emerged—three, cloaked, hands on hilts.

"Travelers," one said, voice smooth as oil. "Lost?"

Gav's charm kicked in. "Just passing through, good sirs. Heading to Marécage for herbs."

The leader laughed. "Herbs. In Vallombre's fog? You're either fools or spies."

Elyse's dagger flashed, but too slow. The men moved like smoke—Greys, Consortium shades. One grabbed her arm; another lunged for Gav.

He ran.

thief-running in fog was madness, but Gav knew madness. He vaulted a fallen log, scrambled up a gorge wall slick with dew, feet finding holds where none seemed. Shouts behind, a curse as Elyse fought. A knife whistled past his ear.

He crested the gorge, dropped into a sunken ruin—Aurelian baths, black water reflecting twisted faces. He splashed through, heart pounding, the fog his ally now.

But the shades were fast. One caught his ankle, yanked him down. Gav kicked, connected with a jaw. "The letter!" the man snarled. "Give it!"

Gav rolled, drew his own knife—a pitiful thing, stolen from a fishmonger. "Come take it."

The fight was brutal, grounded—mud in eyes, fists in guts. Gav's speed saved him; he dodged, slashed, opened a gash on the man's arm. But the shade was trained, ruthless. A boot to Gav's ribs sent him sprawling.

Then, a whisper in the fog: "Enough."

The shade froze. From the mist stepped a woman—elegant, dark-haired, face veiled but eyes piercing. Lady Serina Vex. The Factor herself. No intermediate now; the game had turned imperative.

"You," she said to Gav, voice laced with amusement and threat. "The quick one. You've led us a chase."

Gav gasped for breath, ribs afire. "What do you want?"

"The letter. And you. Talent like yours... wasted on streets."

Charismatic, yes—this thief-queen with her merchant's smile. But ruthless: bodies vanished, empires toppled in shadows.

Elyse stumbled into view, bloodied, guarded by another shade. "He's mine," she spat.

Serina laughed. "Lumière's lapdog. Tell Alain the Consortium claims this prize."

A revelation hit Gav: the letter was bait, yes—but for whom? Consortium planted it to flush out Lumière's spies? Or Beaumont's?

Trust eroded further. He lunged—not at Serina, but the water. Splashed in, swam deep, the letter dissolving? No, wax-sealed still.

He surfaced on the far side, ran again.

Behind, orders: "Alive! He's useful."

The web closed.

### Chapter 4: The Gilded Noose

The roads to Couronne were a misery of mud and mist, churned to slurry by the endless caravans that fed the king's court like blood to a leech. Gav clung to the back of a grain wagon, his fingers numb from the grip, his arse bruised from every rut and stone. Three days since the ruins of Vallombre, three days of dodging shades in the fog, sleeping in ditches that stank of rot and regret. He had run, aye, but running only took a man so far when the hunters knew the paths better than the hare. They had caught him on the second night, silent as ghosts, a cloth over his mouth that smelled of bitter herbs and darkness. When he woke, it was in a cart bound for the capital, bound himself with ropes that bit like accusations.

The man who watched him was no shade, not truly—one of Serina's factors, perhaps, or a bought guard with a face like weathered leather and eyes that promised nothing good. "Lady wants a word," was all he said, and Gav knew better than to argue. His boots still squelched with the black water of those Aurelian baths, his tunic stiff with dried mud, a far cry from the silks and velvets that awaited in Couronne. He had no talisman, no heirloom from a family he never knew—only the letter, crumpled and hidden in the lining of his boot, its words a curse that weighed heavier than any chain. *Pointed ears. The old woman is half-elf.* It was his only weapon now, a scrap of parchment that could burn down baronies or get him gutted in an alley.

Couronne rose from the plains like a crown of thorns, its walls high and white, scarred by old sieges and the endless rains that plagued Merovingia's heart. The gates were choked with folk: merchants hawking spices that burned the nose, pilgrims in rags muttering prayers to gods long forgotten, guards in the king's gold-and-blue livery eyeing every face as if it hid a dagger. Gav's captor cut through it all with a whispered word and a flash of silver—Consortium coin, no doubt—and they passed into the city proper.

The streets were a clash that set Gav's teeth on edge. In Harveston, the mud was honest, the stink of fish and sweat a familiar rot. Here, the cobbles gleamed underfoot, polished by a thousand boots, but the gutters ran with the same filth, hidden beneath arches of Aurelian stone that whispered of glories long crumbled. Marble fountains sprayed water that tasted of minerals and magic—rumors said the old empire's wards still lingered, cursing those who drank too deep with visions of the fall. Gav spat when his captor offered a sip from a skin; he trusted no water in this place, no more than he trusted the smiles of the courtiers who swept past in silks dyed the colors of blood and envy.

They left the wagon in a stable that smelled of hay and horse piss, a grounded scent that eased Gav's jangling nerves for a moment. Then up through twisting alleys, past taverns where laughter spilled like sour ale, to a merchant house that looked plain from the street but opened into opulence within: carpets thick as moss, walls hung with tapestries depicting hunts where the prey had human eyes. Gav's mud-caked boots left prints on the weave, and he ground them in deeper out of spite. Let them see where he came from—the streets, not these gilded cages.

The factor shoved him into a chamber lit by candles that flickered like uneasy lies. "Clean yerself," the man grunted, pointing to a basin of steaming water and a pile of clothes. "Lady don't like filth at her table."

Gav stripped, the water turning brown as he scrubbed away the road's grime. The clothes were servant's livery—grey wool, plain but fine, with no sigil to mark allegiance. Clever, that. He could pass for any house's drudge in the chaos of the Winter Ball. His cynicism twisted like a knife: they meant to use him as a tool, slip him into the palace like a poisoned needle. And for what? To deliver the letter? To steal something worse?

The door opened without a knock, and in stepped a woman—not Serina, but close enough to her shadow. Tall, with hair like polished ebony and eyes that weighed a man's soul in a glance. A intermediate, Gav thought, one of those factors the great ones used to keep their hands clean. She carried a tray with bread, cheese, and a flagon of wine—red as rubies, sour on the tongue but without the bite of tavern swill.

"Eat," she said, her voice smooth as silk over steel. "You'll need strength for the ball."

Gav tore into the bread, hunger overriding wariness. It was fresh, warm, laced with herbs that spoke of kitchens far grander than any he'd known. But he watched her over the crust, cynical thoughts churning: *They feed the lamb before the slaughter.* "What's yer name, then?" he asked, mouth full, letting crumbs fall like accusations. Class tension hung in the air; he was the gutter rat, she the courtly bird.

She smiled, thin and without warmth. "Call me Mira. Lady Serina sends her regards. You've proven quick, boy—quicker than most. That's why you're alive."

Gav swallowed, the cheese cloying on his tongue. "Alive for what? To dance at the king's feast?"

Mira's eyes glittered. "The Winter Ball is more than dances. Kings and barons gather, alliances forged in whispers, knives drawn in shadows. The Beaumonts will be there—Marguerite, Nyra, Garrick. They think the portrait destroyed, the secret buried. But we have copies. You will deliver one—to the king himself, perhaps, or to a tongue that wags in his ear."

Gav's stomach turned. The letter in his boot felt like a brand. "And if I don't?"

Her smile faded. "Then the shades finish what they started in the ruins. But do it well, and Lady Serina might find a place for you. The Consortium rewards talent."

Charismatic words, laced with the promise of coin and safety, but Gav's jaded lens saw the ruthlessness beneath: use him, then discard him like a blunted blade. Major players like Serina acted through intermediates like Mira—until the game turned imperative, and the knife had to be wielded personally. He nodded, feigning eagerness. "Aye, a place sounds fine. Better than ditches."

Mira left him with instructions: slip into the palace as a server, find the Beaumonts' table, listen for the signal—a dropped goblet from a Consortium plant. Then act. Gav lay on the pallet after, staring at the ceiling beams carved with sigils that might ward off curses or invite them. Magic was rumor in Merovingia—elven whispers in Sylvaine's woods, dwarven runes in Rochedur's quarries—but here in Couronne, it felt closer, a subtle curse in the air that made trusts erode like riverbanks.

Night fell like a cloak, the city's bells tolling the hour of the ball. Gav joined a throng of servants herded through the palace's side gates, his grey livery blending with the rest. The contrast hit him like a fist: from mud-slick roads to marble floors that gleamed under chandeliers of crystal and flame. The air smelled of perfume and roasted meats, not piss and decay. Courtiers swirled in silks, their laughter tinkling like breaking glass, house sigils pinned to breasts hiding blood feuds older than the kingdom. Beaumont's wheat and dagger, Lumière's radiant sun, Vallombre's shadowed veil—all here, scheming beneath the boy's crown.

Gav carried a tray of goblets, the wine sloshing like blood. His wide-eyed shock warred with grounded roots: *This is no different from the market,* he told himself. *Just bigger purses to cut.* But the opulence mocked him—the golden plates, the minstrels singing of ancient Aurelian glories, the king himself on his throne, young and petulant, his crown slipping as he laughed at a jester's jape. Clovis IV, bearer of the sacred burden, but Gav saw a boy playing at manhood, surrounded by flatterers who whispered poisons sweeter than wine.

He wove through the crowd, ears pricked for whispers. Near the high table, the Beaumonts gathered: Marguerite frail in her chair, ears veiled by silver hair, but her eyes sharp as thorns. Nyra beside her, face half-shadowed, her quarter-elven grace hidden behind a mask of human poise. Garrick laughed with a merchant, his own pointed ears concealed by tousled locks, but tension lined his smile. They spoke in low tones, subtext thick as fog.

"...the portrait's ash," Nyra murmured. "But Lumière's dogs sniff still. And the Consortium—"

"Presses," Garrick finished. "Serina wants our shadows broken. If the king learns of the blood..."

Marguerite's hand tightened on her cane. "He won't. Our intermediates hold the lines."

Gav's tray trembled. Intermediates—like him, now. He moved closer, offering wine. Nyra took a goblet without glancing, her fingers brushing his—cold, like the mist of Vallombre.

A goblet dropped nearby—clatter of silver on stone. The signal.

Gav's moral dilemma coiled like a serpent: deliver the copy Mira had slipped him, a forged note echoing the letter's truths, and betray the Beaumonts who had offered him shelter? Or warn them, and face Serina's wrath? His grounded roots screamed to run, to vanish into the streets with whatever coin he could snatch. But the intrigue's pull—the promise of a place, the shock of courtly deceit—held him. Trust eroded: Marguerite had used him as bait, Serina as a tool. No heroes here, only charismatic wolves in velvet.

He leaned in, whispering to Nyra, "The wine—don't drink. Consortium's play."

Her eyes flicked to him, recognition dawning. "You. The thief from Thornhaven."

Before he could explain, a hand clamped his shoulder—Mira, her face a mask of calm fury. "Boy," she hissed, subtext dripping venom. "The lady calls."

The imperative had come: Serina acted directly now, stepping from the shadows into the hall's light. She approached, elegant in grey silk, her smile charismatic as a siren's song. "A word, young Gav."

The Beaumonts turned, tension crackling like storm air. Nyra's hand went to a hidden dagger. "Serina Vex. Bold, showing your face here."

Serina's eyes glittered. "The game demands it. The portrait lives, Beaumont. Your elven taint—exposed tonight."

Revelation struck Gav like a blade: the letter, the portrait—all part of a larger web. Serina had planted the original to flush Lumière's spies, but now used copies to strike at the Beaumonts directly. And he, the innocent fool, had carried the thread.

Garrick rose, hand on hilt. "Lies."

But Marguerite's face paled—a subtle curse, perhaps, the rumored elven blood making her visions swim. "The boy... he knows."

Chaos bloomed: a servant—Consortium plant—lunged with a poisoned needle, aimed at Marguerite. Gav shoved her aside, the tray crashing, wine spilling like blood. Shouts echoed, guards drawing steel.

Serina's knife flashed, imperative and ruthless, slicing toward Gav's throat. "Traitor."

He dodged, thief-running instincts saving him—vaulting a table, rolling into the crowd. But the hall was a trap, doors barred for the ball.

A cliffhanger loomed: from the shadows, another figure emerged—Baron Alain de Lumière, radiant in gold, his knights at heel. "Enough," he boomed. "The king demands truth."

But in his hand, a goblet—offered to Clovis, steaming with a subtle curse of its own?

Gav's trust shattered utterly. The web closed, knives in every hand.
